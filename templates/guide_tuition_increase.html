Daycare tuition increase letter: three templates

Raising rates is the letter every provider dreads writing. These three versions — a standard letter, a warmer explainer, and a short text/email note — say the same four things clearly: the new amount, the effective date, the reason, and how to reach you. Copy, replace the brackets, send with real notice.

Prepared by TuitionPing · Updated October 20, 2026

Before you send: notice and timing

Give at least the notice your enrollment agreement or payment policy promises — 30 days is a common example — and pick an effective date families can plan around, usually the first billing period of a new month. Send the letter once, in writing they can keep; repeat the key facts (new amount, date) in your normal communications as the date approaches, but don't drip-feed the number across a month of hints.

One decision to make first: does the increase apply to everyone on the same date, or are current families grandfathered for a period? Either is defensible. Pick one, write it in the letter, and apply it the same way to everyone it covers.

Template 1 — the standard letter

Dear [Parent/Guardian name],

Beginning [effective date], tuition for [child's name / program] will increase from $[current amount] to $[new amount] per [week/month]. All other terms — due date, payment methods and our late-fee policy — stay the same.

This adjustment covers [rising staff, food and supply costs / our updated insurance and licensing costs], so we can keep the care and staffing your child knows.

Please sign and return the acknowledgement below by [date], or contact [name] at [phone/email] with any questions. Thank you for trusting us with [child's name].

Sincerely, [Your name], [Program name]

Acknowledged: ______________________ Date: __________

Template 2 — the warm explainer

Dear families,

Thank you for being part of [Program name]. We are writing early so you can plan: from [effective date], monthly tuition will move from $[current] to $[new].

Over the past year our costs for [teacher wages, meals and classroom supplies] have risen, and this change lets us keep [the same teachers, hours and small groups] without cutting corners anywhere your child would feel it. Nothing else about your bill changes — same due date, same way to pay.

If the new amount creates a problem for your family, please talk to [name] before [date]. We would rather make a written arrangement than lose a family we love over a letter. Please return the signed acknowledgement by [date].

With appreciation, [Your name]

Use this version when the increase is larger, or when many of your families are price-sensitive. The invitation to talk is real — only include it if you will honor it, and put any arrangement you agree in writing, as the late-payment follow-up guide describes.

Template 3 — the short version (text or email)

English

Hi [Parent], a quick note from [Program]: tuition will be $[new amount] per [month] starting [effective date]. The full letter is [attached / in your child's cubby]. Nothing else changes. Questions? Reply here. Thank you!

Español

Hola [Padre/Madre], una nota breve de [Programa]: la cuota será de $[nuevo monto] por [mes] a partir del [fecha de entrada en vigor]. La carta completa está [adjunta / en el casillero de su hijo]. Nada más cambia. ¿Preguntas? Responda a este mensaje. ¡Gracias!

The short version is a heads-up, not a substitute for the letter. Send both: the text so it is actually read, the letter so the family has the terms in writing.

Mistakes that make increases harder than they need to be

  • Apologizing through the whole letter. One warm line is enough; a page of apology invites negotiation on a decision you have already made.
  • Hiding the new amount. If a parent has to calculate a percentage to learn their new bill, the letter has failed. State both numbers.
  • No effective date, or several. One date, stated once, matching your policy's notice rule.
  • Changing other terms silently. If the due date or late fee is also changing, say so in the same letter — don't let a reminder text be where a family learns it.
  • Forgetting your own records. Update each family's amount before the effective date, or your next reminders will quote the old tuition.

After you send it

Collect acknowledgements, file them, and update the amount on every family's record with the effective date. If you use reminder texts, the new amount should appear from the first bill at the new rate — in TuitionPing that is a single edit per family, and the schedule (-3 days, due date, +3, +7) carries on unchanged. Finish by updating the amount in your payment policy handout so new families enrolling after the change sign the current number.

Common questions

How much notice should a daycare give for a tuition increase?

At least the notice your enrollment agreement or payment policy promises — 30 days is a common example. More notice is kinder for a larger increase; whatever you choose, state the exact effective date in the letter.

What should a tuition increase letter include?

The current amount, the new amount, the exact effective date, a brief honest reason, what is not changing, how to acknowledge or ask questions, and a thank-you. One page is enough.

Should I explain why tuition is increasing?

Yes, briefly and concretely — staff wages, food and supply costs, insurance. A specific reason respects families' budgets; a long financial defense usually backfires.

Do families need to sign the increase letter?

An acknowledgement line protects both sides: it shows the family received the new amount and date before it took effect. Keep signed copies with each child's file and update your billing records to the new amount on the effective date.

Can I send the increase by text message?

A short text works as the heads-up, but follow it with the full letter families can keep. The text should name the new amount and effective date and point to the letter, not replace it.
